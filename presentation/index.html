Our thesis is Reading the Reader: a modular software architecture for adaptive reading systems. Over the next twenty-five minutes or so, we'll walk you through the problem we set out to solve, the platform we built for it, and what we found. The problem

Reading is hard for some readers

For readers with dyslexia or age-related vision loss, text can demand more than they can comfortably process.

Digital text can reshape itself as it is read, and an eye tracker can tell when a reader is struggling, so it could adapt in real time without breaking the reader's flow.

Imagine a reader with dyslexia, or someone losing their central vision with age. For them, a page of text can demand more effort than they can comfortably give. On the right is just a simulation of what that can feel like: for central vision loss the middle of the page falls away, and for a dyslexic reader the letters won't sit still. Now, here is the opportunity: digital text is not fixed. It can reshape itself while it is being read. And an eye tracker can tell us when a reader is struggling, in the moment. So in principle, the text could adapt in real time, to that specific reader. But there is one hard constraint, and it runs through everything we built: the adaptation must never cost the reader their place, or their flow. The instant it does, we've made reading harder, not easier. This isn't a new idea in our group, and that's exactly where the real problem starts.

The concept, and what is missing

Easy to draw, hard to earn

The classifier is the hard part. It is trained on a database of readers, so someone must first produce that data. And the decider can be a human (a researcher) or an AI.

But there is no platform to run the research. Prior prototypes proved the concept, yet were too coupled to swap pieces in and out.

Does wider letter spacing help an AMD reader resume faster?

Does a highlight cue cut regressions?

Each hypothesis needs different sensing, intervention, and decision logic, and a researcher just wants to plug in a module and gather data.

Concept figure reproduced from the Reading the Reader programme, DTU Compute.

This is the concept as the programme publishes it. A reader is sensed by an eye tracker, which gives us a raw stream of gaze points. Those points are turned into features, measures that actually describe how someone is reading, like how long the eye rests on a word, or when it jumps back to re-read. A classifier then reads those features and decides, and an intervention adapts the text, closing the loop. Now look at that classifier sitting in the middle, because it quietly hides two things that matter. First, it is trained on a database of readers, so before you even have a classifier, someone has to produce that data, cleanly and reproducibly. Second, the decider does not have to be an AI. In the loop, it can just as easily be a human, a researcher, making the call. Now the gap. To learn what actually helps, a researcher runs many hypotheses, and each one needs different sensing, a different intervention, a different decision rule. Does wider letter spacing help an AMD reader resume faster? Does a highlight cue cut regressions? Different logic every time. Prior prototypes in the programme proved the concept works, but they were too coupled to swap those pieces in and out. There was simply no platform to do this research. So I will hand over to Sachin, who will show how we turned this concept into an architecture.

[reveal Sensing] The first component is Sensing. This is where the hardware meets the system: the eye tracker produces raw gaze samples, and Sensing is the part that receives them, whether they come from a real Tobii device or from a simulated source.

[reveal Analysis] The second is Analysis. On their diagram this was feature extraction. Analysis is where raw gaze stops being coordinates and becomes reading behaviour: fixations, saccades, and regressions, the oculomotor events that actually describe how someone is reading.

[reveal Decision] The third is Decision. On their diagram this was the classifier. Decision is the part that looks at those reading events and identifies struggle: it decides whether an intervention is needed right now, and if so, proposes one.

[reveal Intervention] The fourth is Intervention. This is the part that actually changes the text the reader sees.

[connect the four] And now that all four are up, look at how they connect. The output of each one feeds the next: gaze is sensed, sensing feeds analysis, analysis feeds the decision, the decision drives an intervention, and the changed text feeds straight back into how the person reads. That closed cycle is what we call the adaptive loop: one live reading session, running continuously.

[reveal provider seam] Now the important part, and the reason we bothered to name four components instead of one. These four are exactly the pieces that have to be interchangeable. A researcher has to be able to swap one out without rewriting the other three. And the sharpest example is Decision. We do not build the final classifier in this thesis. We turn it into a provider that plugs into a decision seam, and that provider can be a human researcher, a rule-based strategy, or later an AI model. So replaceability is not something we bolted on afterwards; it is what the whole decomposition was for.

Deep dive · Context preservation (RQ3)

Does changing the text throw the reader off?

This was the result we most wanted to know. Every reflow risks losing the reader's place, so we measured it: four participants, the same article, with and without the restore, aligned to each intervention.

Thanks, Sachin. So the architecture holds together. Now let's look at the piece of evidence I find most useful, because this is the one thing a reader would actually feel. The question we most wanted to answer was simple: when the system changes the text while someone is in the middle of reading, does it make them lose their place? Every time you reflow a page, you risk exactly that, so we measured it directly. We looked at two things each time an intervention fired. The first is how long it took the reader to get going again, the little pause between the text changing and their eyes moving forward into the next word. With our context-preserving feature turned on, that pause was about 482 milliseconds. With it turned off, it was 650. So keeping the reader's place got them reading again noticeably faster. The second thing we looked at is how often the reader's eyes jumped backwards to re-read, which is a classic sign of confusion or disruption. In the five seconds right after a change, with preservation that dropped to 23 percent, actually below the reader's own normal rate. Without it, it stayed up at 33 percent. And the same pattern held whether we looked at a three-, five-, or ten-second window afterwards. Now, one honest thing to be clear about: this was four participants, so these are descriptive results, a clear and consistent direction, not a formal, statistically proven effect. I'll come back to that in a moment. So that is what the platform can do. Now, honestly, let me tell you what it cannot.

Honest boundaries

What we did not prove

We state these ourselves. They bound what the results claim without undercutting them, and each one points to a next step.

  • Effect No efficacy claim We can apply and measure interventions, not yet show that any of them helps. Small, non-clinical sample.
  • AI decision-making Architectural, not built The provider seam ran end to end on real hardware, but with a fixed-rule stand-in, not a learned model.
  • Context restore Fixed, not yet re-tested The over-repositioning is fixed geometrically; a re-test with real readers is still pending.
So let me be honest about the other side: the things we did not prove. Being clear about these limits actually makes what we did prove stronger, not weaker. There are three, and the first is the one I just flagged. First, effect. We showed that the platform can make a change to the text and measure what happens right after, but we did not show that any particular change actually helps someone read better. And remember, we only had four readers, and they were people we could conveniently recruit, not the older or dyslexic readers this is really meant for. So the proper study, the one that asks whether this genuinely helps, is something we deliberately left as future work. Second, the AI. When we talk about an AI making the decisions, that part is architecture, not a finished, trained model. We did run the full decision pathway end to end on the real eye tracker, but the thing making the calls was a simple set of fixed rules standing in for a real AI. And we only ran that automatic path once, as a check, not as a full study. Third, the restore, the feature that keeps the reader's place when the text changes. Our first version actually overshot on small changes, and nudged the reader a little past where the text had really moved. We have since fixed that in the geometry. It is a small, contained fix, and the only thing still open is a fresh round of testing with readers to confirm it. The good news is that every one of these points straight at a clear next step. (If asked about how we keep gaze locked to the right line: it is a hand-tuned bias, a poor man's Kalman filter, borrowing the intuition but not the machinery. Full detail is on backup card B2.)

Backup · B1

Fixation detection: I-AOI (dwell-based)

The spatial stage already resolves each gaze point to the word it falls on, so the word token is the area of interest, and a fixation is identified purely from the dwell time on it.

  • The I-AOI branch of the Salvucci and Goldberg taxonomy. NOT I-DT (dispersion), NOT I-VT (velocity). There is no velocity threshold.
  • A token is held as a candidate, then confirmed as a fixation once dwell passes 90 ms initially, 70 ms within a line, and 135 ms across a line change (raised because that move is larger and noisier).
  • Per-word binning by total dwell: under 45 ms is dropped as noise, at least 130 ms counts as a fixation, and in between is recorded as a skim.
THE answer to have cold. Dwell/area-of-interest based (I-AOI in Salvucci–Goldberg), NOT I-DT, NOT I-VT. There is NO velocity threshold. Report the real dwell-time constants. Both presenters must be able to say this instantly.

Backup · B2

The line-bias vs. a Kalman filter

A deterministic hysteresis bias, not a Kalman filter. It keeps gaze on the line being read despite vertical jitter of the order of the line spacing.

  • In pickBestLine, each sample is charged for its vertical distance to every line band; the line already being read gets a fixed discount, so jitter does not flicker the mapping onto a neighbour.
  • It shares a Kalman filter's intuition (use the prior position to reject a single noisy sample) but none of its machinery: no probabilistic state, no covariance, no predict-and-update cycle.
  • A principled Kalman filter would generalise it, and is future work. We do not claim it is one.
Deterministic hysteresis/bias on the current line to reject single-sample vertical jitter. Shares a Kalman filter's intuition (use prior position), NOT its probabilistic machinery. A real Kalman filter generalises it, future work. Do not overclaim it "is" a Kalman filter.

Backup · B3

AOI survives an intervention's reflow

Yes, this is handled, and it is a deliberate piece of engineering rather than a gap.

  • Word boxes are re-measured from the live DOM on every mutation, resize, and scroll.
  • So after an intervention reflows the text, the gaze-to-word mapping uses fresh coordinates, never stale ones.
  • Gaze is normalised before mapping (FR4.2), so the boxes hold across screen resolutions too.
"We already solved that concern": word boxes are re-measured from the live DOM on every mutation / resize / scroll, so the mapping uses fresh coordinates after a reflow.

Backup · B4

Design decision register (DR1–DR9)

Every architectural choice is traced to the driver that forced it; the trade-offs are deliberate and visible.

  • DR1 Inward-dependency layering (ports and adapters)
  • DR2 Uniform seam shape with a per-concern registry
  • DR3 Dedicated real-time channel for gaze
  • DR4 Uniform, identity-keyed provider seam
  • DR5 Two-phase validate-then-commit intervention
  • DR6 One authoritative, schema-versioned record
  • DR7 File-backed, checkpointed local store
  • DR8 Sensing-source port behind an adapter
  • DR9 Autonomous and advisory execution modes

DR3, DR7, and the DR4 fallback trade a measure of architectural purity for responsiveness and robustness; DR1, DR2, and DR8 hold the separation of concerns firm.

For any "why did you choose X" question: the trade-offs, each against its driver. DR3/DR7/DR4-fallback trade purity for responsiveness/robustness; DR1/DR2/DR8 hold separation firm, deliberate, visible.

Backup · B5

Tech stack + Tobii SDK integration

  • Frontend: Next.js 16, React 19, TypeScript 5, Bun; RTK Query and Tailwind v4.
  • Backend: C# on .NET 10, ASP.NET Core, FastEndpoints 8; CsvHelper for record export.
  • Realtime: gaze on a dedicated WebSocket channel (/ws); commands over REST (/api).
