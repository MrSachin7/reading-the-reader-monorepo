\section{Context Preservation}
\label{sec:eval-context}

The third research question asks how a typographic micro-intervention can change the text while preserving the reader's context.
The eight sessions form a with-and-without comparison: in four of them the context-preserving restore is enabled, and in four it is disabled.
In the enabled condition the restore ran with its highlight cue active, so that condition pairs the positional restore with a brief highlight of the restored sentence (\cref{sec:impl-context}), whereas the disabled condition applies neither; the two are therefore contrasted as a bundle rather than as the restore geometry in isolation, a confound we record in \cref{sec:eval-threats}.
We report two behavioural measures aligned to each intervention, and then the mechanism's own per-intervention record from the four sessions in which it ran.

A regression, a backward saccade against the reading direction, is a standard oculomotor sign of reading disruption \cite{rayner1998eyemovements}.
Because context preservation acts only in the moments after a reflow, we aligned the measures to each intervention rather than averaging over the session: for every applied intervention we measured the reading-resume time, the time to the first forward saccade after it, and the regression rate in the five seconds after it against a matched five-second baseline before it.
Aligning to interventions rather than to sessions leaves the two conditions with unequal counts: the four preservation-enabled sessions contributed nineteen interventions and the four disabled sessions twenty-six (\cref{tab:eval-sessions}), so the conditions plotted in \cref{fig:eval-rrt,fig:eval-postreg} hold different numbers of points.
The reading-resume time was shorter with preservation than without, with a median of \SI{482}{\milli\second} against \SI{650}{\milli\second} and a mean of \SI{670}{\milli\second} against \SI{923}{\milli\second} (\cref{fig:eval-rrt}).
The regression rate showed the same separation: in the five seconds after an intervention it was 23\% with preservation against 33\% without, having fallen from a 29\% baseline with preservation while staying near its 32\% baseline without (\cref{fig:eval-postreg}).
This pattern held across three-, five-, and ten-second windows, and the resume-time direction held for three of the four participants.
We state the direction of these numbers without drawing an inference from them, as four participants and an unevenly split sample of this size do not support one.

Within the four sessions in which preservation ran, the reader recorded an outcome for each intervention, namely the displacement the reflow induced (the shift of the reader's position before any compensation) and the residual after the restore, together with a graded status.
Most of the nineteen interventions were layout-changing (a font-size, line-width, or line-height change), which trigger the semantic-restart strategy, and one was an appearance-only change.
Three interventions were graded preserved and sixteen degraded.
For the degraded interventions the median residual was 389\,px, while the median induced displacement was only 38\,px.
\Cref{fig:eval-overreposition} plots the residual against the induced displacement for each intervention, and the points lie above the line of equality, that is, the restore moved the reader's position further than the reflow itself had.
This is the measured behaviour of the \emph{original} semantic-restart strategy on small layout reflows; it motivated a revision to the restore that we evaluate with the deterministic sweep below and examine further in \cref{ch:discussion}.
Each outcome, including its graded status, is written into the session record, so the cost of every intervention to the reader's continuity is auditable after the fact.

The per-intervention record above is observational: each outcome comes from whichever interventions the four participants happened to receive, so it covers neither every intervention type nor a controlled range of magnitudes.
To measure how far an intervention actually moves the reader's position, and whether preservation reduces that movement, we drove the restore mechanism over a deterministic sweep with preservation first off and then on.
A measurement harness mounts the participant reader (\cref{sec:impl-context}) outside a live session, renders the document at the baseline typography, and fires each built-in layout intervention from every page of the paginated document in both conditions, recording the on-screen displacement of the word the reader was on (the change in that word's offset from the top of the reading area across the intervention).
Because the harness exercises the same \idtt{usePreserveReadingContext} path as a live session, the displacement it reports is the mechanism's own rather than a reimplementation, and the harness and its analysis are committed with the source so the figures are reproducible.
The eleven layout interventions crossed with six reading positions yield 66 trials per condition.

\Cref{tab:eval-displacement} reports the word's displacement under three conditions, the median over the six reading positions: without preservation, with the original restore, and with the revised restore.
Without preservation the reflow moved the reader's word by a median of \SI{34}{px}, about one line and of the same order as the \SI{38}{px} seen in the live sessions, though the effect was heavy-tailed: a six-point font enlargement moved it by \SI{142}{px} (over four lines), and a line-width reduction, which also repaginates the document, by \SI{188}{px}.
This is the disruption the restore must absorb.

With the original restore strategy, which re-established the committed sentence at the top of the reading area, preservation made the displacement markedly worse rather than better.
The word's median displacement rose to \SI{104}{px}, three times the no-preservation figure, and the restore moved the word further than the bare reflow in 45 of the 66 trials, the over-repositioning of \cref{fig:eval-overreposition} reproduced under controlled conditions.
The effect was starkest for the smallest changes: a two-point font step that moves the word only \SI{24}{px} on its own was thrown \SI{168}{px}, and a letter-spacing change that moves it not at all was thrown \SI{115}{px}, because every change snapped the committed sentence to the top regardless of how far the reader actually sat from it.
This is why we revised the restore.

The revised restore returns the captured reading position to the on-screen offset it held before the change (\cref{sec:impl-context}).
It removes the over-repositioning, moving the word further than the reflow in only 4 of the 66 trials, and brings the median displacement back to \SI{32}{px}, at or below the no-preservation baseline.
The improvement is concentrated where the reflow is large: the six-point enlargement reads \SI{142}{px} without preservation, \SI{225}{px} with the original restore, and \SI{43}{px} with the revised one, whereas interventions that already move the word by about a line or less return to roughly their no-preservation value, a font reduction is left marginally worse, and line-width changes repaginate and lie outside what a within-page restore can compensate.
The mechanism's own graded residual, measured against the sentence the restore realigns rather than against the word, is correspondingly more favourable (a median of \SI{31}{px} with 61 of 66 outcomes graded preserved, against \SI{138}{px} and 16 of 66 for the original strategy); we read the stricter word-level displacement of \cref{tab:eval-displacement} as the primary measure and return to both in \cref{ch:discussion}.

\Cref{fig:eval-overreposition-fixed} places both restore versions on the axes of \cref{fig:eval-overreposition}: the original residual sits above the line of equality across the sweep, while the revised residual returns onto or below it, with the few remaining overshoots confined to small displacements near the origin.
